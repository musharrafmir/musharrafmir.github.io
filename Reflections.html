Week 3

AI and Originality

This week made me reflect more deeply on the concept of originality, especially in the context of using AI tools in academic work. At first, I saw AI mainly as a productivity tool. However, after using it more actively, I began to notice how easily it can replace parts of my own thinking process. This raised an important concern for me: if AI can generate well-structured and convincing text quickly, what is left for me to contribute as a researcher?

I realized that originality is not only about producing something new, but about developing a personal understanding and interpretation of ideas. AI can reorganize existing knowledge, but it does not experience doubt, curiosity, or intellectual struggle. These elements are central to how humans generate original insights. Without engaging in that process, it becomes difficult to claim ownership over the ideas we present.

At the same time, I do not think AI necessarily reduces originality. Instead, it changes how originality is expressed. It can support early stages of thinking, such as brainstorming or structuring, but it should not replace deeper reflection. For me, the challenge is to use AI in a way that enhances my thinking rather than weakening it. This requires conscious effort, especially when it is tempting to accept AI-generated answers as final.

What is AGI? How does it affect scientific research?

Artificial General Intelligence (AGI) refers to a form of AI that can perform a wide range of cognitive tasks at a level comparable to human intelligence. Unlike current AI systems, which are designed for specific functions, AGI would be capable of learning across domains, adapting to new problems, and making complex decisions.

If AGI becomes a reality, it could significantly transform scientific research. Many current research tasks, such as data analysis, literature review, and even hypothesis generation, could be automated or enhanced. This would increase efficiency and potentially accelerate the pace of discovery. However, this also raises important questions about the role of human researchers.

Scientific research is not only about processing information. It involves interpretation, contextual understanding, and value-based judgment. For example, in public policy research, decisions are influenced by social, political, and ethical considerations that cannot be reduced to data alone. Even if AGI can identify patterns, it may not fully understand their meaning in real-world contexts.

Therefore, while AGI could improve technical aspects of research, it is unlikely to replace the need for human judgment. Instead, it may shift the role of researchers from data processing to critical evaluation and decision-making.

Week 4

How to start using AI for your project?

This week helped me understand that using AI effectively requires more than simply asking questions. The quality of AI output depends heavily on how clearly the research problem is defined. When prompts are vague, the responses tend to be general and not very useful. This made me realize that AI does not replace the need for a clear research design; in fact, it makes it even more important.

A structured approach to using AI is more effective. For example, I found it useful to: - begin with a clear research question
- use AI to explore existing literature and identify key themes
- refine prompts based on initial responses
- critically evaluate and edit the output

This process turns AI into an interactive tool rather than a one-time solution. It also ensures that I remain actively engaged in the research process.

Which AI models are you using? How can they support your research?

I mainly use ChatGPT and Copilot, and I have noticed that they complement each other in useful ways. ChatGPT is particularly strong in generating explanations and simplifying complex ideas. It helps me quickly understand unfamiliar topics and develop initial drafts. Copilot, on the other hand, is more structured and sometimes more precise, especially for coding or organizing content.

Using multiple models also highlights their limitations. For example, they may produce different answers to the same question, which shows that their outputs are not always reliable. This reinforces the need for verification and critical thinking.

In my research, I use AI for: - summarizing articles
- organizing ideas
- drafting initial text

However, I always revise the output and connect it to my own understanding. This helps maintain both accuracy and originality.

Week 5

How do humans find knowledge? How is knowledge different from information?

This week clarified an important distinction between information and knowledge. Information consists of facts or data, but knowledge requires interpretation and understanding. Humans transform information into knowledge through processes such as reasoning, questioning, and connecting different ideas.

AI can provide large amounts of information very quickly, but it does not truly understand what it produces. It lacks context, experience, and the ability to reflect. This means that AI can assist in gathering information, but the process of creating knowledge remains a human activity.

This distinction is especially important in academic research. Simply collecting information is not enough; meaningful research requires analysis and interpretation.

AI discrepancies and failures

While working with AI, I observed several consistent patterns of failure. These are not random mistakes but systematic limitations of the technology.

Some common issues include: - hallucination, where the model generates incorrect or fabricated information
- bias, reflecting patterns present in the training data
- lack of depth when dealing with complex or abstract topics

One important observation is that AI often presents answers with confidence, even when they are incorrect. This can be misleading, especially for users who are not familiar with the topic.

These issues highlight the importance of: - verifying information using reliable sources
- comparing outputs from different models
- maintaining a critical perspective

AI is a powerful tool, but it cannot replace careful evaluation.

Week 6

Starting the first ML pipeline

Developing a machine learning pipeline provided a clearer understanding of how models are built and evaluated. The process involves several stages, including data preparation, feature selection, model training, and evaluation.

What became clear is that the quality of the model depends heavily on the quality of the data and the choices made during preprocessing. Small issues, such as missing values or poorly defined variables, can have a large impact on results.

This experience showed that machine learning is not just about algorithms. It is a structured process where each step contributes to the final outcome.

What did error analysis reveal? Overfitting and knowledge vs noise

Error analysis provided insights that accuracy alone could not capture. A model may appear to perform well based on accuracy, but still make systematic errors that are not immediately visible.

By examining errors, it becomes possible to identify whether the model is learning meaningful patterns or simply memorizing the data. This is directly related to overfitting. An overfitted model captures noise rather than true structure, which reduces its ability to generalize.

This reflects a broader distinction between knowledge and noise. Knowledge represents stable and meaningful patterns, while noise represents random variation. Understanding this difference is essential for building reliable models.

Week 7

Prediction vs explanation

This week emphasized the difference between prediction and explanation. In some contexts, accurate prediction is sufficient. However, in many research and policy settings, understanding causal relationships is more important.

Prediction answers the question “what will happen,” while explanation addresses “why it happens.” These are fundamentally different goals. For example, predicting income levels may be useful, but understanding the causes of income inequality is necessary for policy design.

This distinction highlights the importance of aligning methods with research objectives.

Causal diagram and confounders

In my research area, the relationship between education and income is influenced by several other variables. These include family background, location, and individual ability. These variables act as confounders and can distort the observed relationship.

A causal diagram helps visualize these relationships and identify which variables need to be controlled. Without accounting for confounders, it is not possible to make valid causal claims.

This exercise reinforced the importance of careful research design and the limitations of simple correlations.

Week 8

Text mining vs close reading

Text mining allows researchers to analyze large amounts of text and identify patterns at scale. This is particularly useful when working with large datasets.

However, it cannot fully replace close reading. Close reading provides depth, context, and interpretation that automated methods cannot capture. Both approaches are necessary for comprehensive analysis.

NLP and LLM in research

NLP and LLMs are valuable tools for research. They can assist with summarizing literature, identifying themes, and organizing information. This can significantly improve efficiency.

However, their outputs must be used carefully. They may miss important details or introduce errors. Therefore, they should be combined with human analysis.

Limitations and solutions

AI tools are limited by bias, hallucination, and lack of interpretability. Addressing these issues requires both technical solutions and careful usage.

Key practices include: - verifying outputs
- using multiple sources
- maintaining critical evaluation

AI for research guide

  • Use AI as a support tool rather than a replacement
  • Always verify important information
  • Avoid over-reliance on a single output
  • Be aware of ethical and academic concerns
  • Maintain active engagement with the research process

Week 9

What can LLMs do well, and where do they fail?

Large Language Models (LLMs) are highly effective at generating fluent text, summarizing information, and explaining concepts in an accessible way. In my experience, they are particularly useful for early-stage research tasks, such as drafting outlines, organizing ideas, and simplifying complex readings. They can also quickly synthesize information from multiple domains, which makes them valuable for interdisciplinary work. However, their strengths are closely tied to their weaknesses. One major limitation is hallucination, where the model produces information that is incorrect but presented in a confident and convincing way. I have encountered situations where the explanation sounded very logical, but upon verification, the details were inaccurate or incomplete. This is especially problematic in academic work, where precision and accuracy are essential. Another issue is that LLMs do not truly “reason” in the human sense; instead, they rely on pattern recognition. As a result, they may struggle with tasks that require deep logical consistency or nuanced interpretation. Overall, LLMs are powerful tools for support, but they require careful supervision and verification.

How might generative AI reshape your discipline?

Generative AI has the potential to significantly reshape how research is conducted in my field over the next five years. It is likely to reduce the time required for tasks such as literature review, data exploration, and drafting written work. This increased efficiency could allow researchers to focus more on higher-level thinking and analysis. However, this shift also raises important concerns about the quality of research. If researchers rely too heavily on AI-generated content, there is a risk of producing work that lacks depth or originality. Additionally, the widespread use of AI may lead to more standardized outputs, reducing diversity in academic perspectives. In my view, the most affected tasks will be those that involve routine processing, such as summarization and formatting. In contrast, tasks that require critical thinking, theoretical development, and interpretation should remain human-driven. These aspects of research depend on judgment, context, and experience, which AI cannot fully replicate. Therefore, generative AI is likely to change how research is done, but not what makes research valuable.

Week 10

What is Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) and why does it matter for research?

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) is an approach that combines language models with external data sources to improve the quality and reliability of generated outputs. Instead of relying solely on pre-trained knowledge, the model retrieves relevant documents and uses them to generate responses. This is particularly important in research contexts, where accuracy and credibility are critical. By grounding responses in real sources, RAG reduces the likelihood of hallucination and increases trust in the output. It also allows users to trace information back to its origin, which is essential for academic work. In addition, RAG enables the use of up-to-date information, which is often missing in standard language models. This makes it especially useful for rapidly evolving fields. However, the effectiveness of RAG depends on the quality of the retrieval process. If the retrieved documents are not relevant or reliable, the final output will also be limited. Overall, RAG represents an important step toward making AI more useful for research applications.

What are the recent criticisms about RAG? Any suggestions for improvement?

Despite its advantages, RAG has received several criticisms, particularly regarding its reliability and consistency. One major issue is that the retrieval component does not always select the most relevant or high-quality documents. This can lead to outputs that are incomplete or misleading. Another concern is that the integration of retrieved information is not always seamless. The model may combine information from different sources in a way that lacks coherence or introduces contradictions. Additionally, RAG systems can be computationally expensive, especially when dealing with large datasets. There are also concerns about transparency, as users may not always know how the retrieved information influenced the final output. To improve RAG systems, several steps can be taken. First, retrieval methods can be enhanced by using better ranking algorithms and filtering mechanisms. Second, models can be trained to better integrate and summarize retrieved information. Third, providing clearer citations and source explanations would improve transparency. These improvements could make RAG more reliable and more suitable for academic use.

Week 11

What is a knowledge graph and how does it differ from a traditional database or a vector store?

A knowledge graph is a data structure that represents information through entities and the relationships between them. Unlike traditional databases, which store data in tables, knowledge graphs focus on connections and interactions. This makes them particularly useful for understanding complex systems where relationships are important. In contrast, vector stores represent data as numerical embeddings and are primarily used for similarity search. While vector stores are useful for retrieval tasks, they do not explicitly capture relationships between entities. Knowledge graphs, on the other hand, allow for more structured and interpretable analysis. They make it possible to query not only individual data points but also the connections between them. This is especially valuable in knowledge mining, where understanding relationships is essential. For example, a knowledge graph can show how different variables influence each other, rather than treating them as independent observations. This makes it a more powerful tool for certain types of research.

Identify three entities and three relationships from your research domain. How would this structure help answer questions?

In my research domain, I can identify entities such as education, occupation, and income. These entities are connected through relationships that reflect real-world dynamics. For example, education influences occupation, occupation determines income, and education can also have a direct effect on income. Representing these connections as a knowledge graph allows for a more comprehensive understanding of how these variables interact. Instead of analyzing each variable separately, the graph shows the structure of their relationships. This makes it easier to identify indirect effects and complex pathways. For instance, it becomes possible to analyze how changes in education affect income through occupation. A flat dataset would not easily capture these interactions. Additionally, knowledge graphs can support more advanced queries, such as identifying which factors have the strongest influence on a particular outcome. This demonstrates how relational structures can provide deeper insights than traditional data formats.

Week 12

What does “AI for Science” mean?

AI for Science refers to the use of artificial intelligence methods to support and enhance scientific discovery. This includes tasks such as analyzing large datasets, building predictive models, and generating new hypotheses. One of the main advantages of AI is its ability to process information at a scale that is not possible for humans. This allows researchers to explore complex systems and identify patterns that would otherwise remain hidden. However, AI does not replace the need for scientific reasoning. It can suggest patterns, but it cannot fully interpret their meaning or significance. This requires human expertise and judgment. In addition, the use of AI in science raises important questions about transparency and reproducibility. Researchers must ensure that AI-generated results can be understood and validated. Therefore, AI for Science should be viewed as a tool that enhances research rather than replaces it.

How do agentic AI systems change the research workflow?

Agentic AI systems represent a shift from simple prompt-based interactions to more complex and autonomous processes. These systems can plan tasks, use tools, and perform multi-step reasoning. This allows them to handle more advanced workflows, such as conducting literature searches, analyzing data, and generating reports. As a result, they can significantly improve research efficiency. However, this increased autonomy also introduces new challenges. For example, it becomes more difficult to track how decisions are made within the system. This raises concerns about transparency and accountability. Additionally, errors at one stage of the process can propagate through the system, affecting the final outcome. Because of this, human oversight remains essential. Researchers must guide the process, verify results, and ensure that conclusions are valid. Agentic AI has the potential to transform research workflows, but it requires careful integration to avoid unintended consequences.
